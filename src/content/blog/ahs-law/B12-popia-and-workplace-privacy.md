Most of us spend a large part of our day connected. Work emails, group chats, and personal WhatsApps blur together, especially when people use work phones or check messages during office hours. The practical question that keeps coming up is simple: how much of that can your employer lawfully see, and what happens if they use it against you?

The answer sits at the intersection of four areas of law: the constitutional right to privacy, the Protection of Personal Information Act 4 of 2013 (POPIA), the Regulation of Interception of Communications and Provision of Communication-Related Information Act 70 of 2002 (RICA), and the Labour Relations Act 66 of 1995 (LRA). None of them gives employers a free hand, and none of them leaves employees without protection.

### The basic legal picture

Section 14 of the Constitution protects the privacy of communications. That right is not absolute in the workplace — the expectation of privacy is lower when you are using company systems — but it does not disappear.

POPIA regulates the processing of personal information. Messages that contain names, contact details, opinions, health information or anything that can identify a person count as personal information. Any access, storage or use of those messages must therefore meet POPIA’s conditions for lawful processing (including purpose limitation, minimality and transparency).

RICA regulates the actual interception of communications. Interception is generally prohibited unless it falls within a recognised exception. The most relevant workplace exception is the “business purpose” provision in section 6: an employer (as system controller) may intercept communications on its own systems if the system is used wholly or partly for the business, the interception is for legitimate business reasons (such as establishing facts, security or authorised use), and the employer has made reasonable efforts to inform users in advance that interceptions may occur.
